# Agora: ethical sales research

Research checked 2 November 2026. This document distinguishes empirical findings, professional ethical commitments, regulator guidance, and proposed teaching design. All people, organizations, prices, product results, and dialogue in the scenarios below are invented for practice. They are not research findings or claims about JobMachine.

## Teaching position

Sales is helping someone decide whether a proposed exchange serves a real need, then making any agreement clear enough to deliver. Competence includes listening, asking, testing fit, explaining value, negotiating terms, hearing no, and keeping promises. Ethics is present in each decision rather than reserved for a final warning lesson.

Agora should reward a sound process even when the appropriate outcome is no sale. A seller can protect their time, margin, and boundaries while respecting the buyer's choice. Honest persuasion can include a clear recommendation, evidence, and a direct invitation to proceed. It need not mean passivity or disclosure of every private reservation price. It must not mean deception, manufactured urgency, invasive questioning, or making refusal costly.

The course's ethical standard is an authored commitment informed by the sources below. The cited studies do not demonstrate that every ethical act increases revenue. Some honest decisions cost a deal.

## Evidence and source register

The summaries in this section are the verified source claims. Later curriculum and scenario sections are original teaching proposals, not validated interventions.

### S1. Questions and responsiveness: a bounded experimental finding

Huang, K., Yeomans, M., Brooks, A. W., Minson, J., and Gino, F. (2017), *It Doesn't Hurt to Ask: Question-Asking Increases Liking*. Primary paper.

https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/Huang%20et%20al%202017_6945bc5e-3b3e-4c0a-addd-254c9e603c60.pdf

**Verified principle:** In experimental introductory conversations, asking more questions, especially follow-up questions, was associated with greater perceived responsiveness and liking. The paper also examined speed-dating conversations.

**Limits:** These are not sales-conversion experiments. The naturalistic speed-dating analysis was challenged by S2. Do not claim a question quota, a universal effect, or a percentage increase in sales. A question can be irrelevant, intrusive, or performative.

### S2. Question asking and listening: a methodological debate

Kluger, A. N., and Malloy, T. E. (2019), *Question Asking as a Dyadic Behavior*. Primary methodological commentary and reanalysis.

https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6857721/

**Verified principle:** The authors challenge how the speed-dating study handled dyadic effects and the use of second-date offers as a proxy for liking. They distinguish question asking from listening; the contested naturalistic result should not be treated as settled evidence of the former causing the latter.

**Limits:** This critique does not make the controlled conversation experiments disappear. It does prevent teaching an uncomplicated “more questions always wins” rule. Teach attention to the answer and adaptation to the person, not merely question frequency.

### S3. Listening skills: paraphrase, acknowledge, clarify

Lisa Dicker and Shane Hebel, Harvard Negotiation & Mediation Clinical Program, *Remote but Robust: having difficult conversations virtually using best practices from crisis counseling*. Expert practice guidance.

https://hnmcp.law.harvard.edu/hnmcp/blog/remote-but-robust-having-difficult-conversations-virtually-using-best-practices-from-crisis-counseling/

**Verified principle:** The guidance recommends listening, reflecting another person's meaning, acknowledging experience, asking for clarification, and collaborating on next steps. Acknowledging a feeling does not require agreeing with a position.

**Limits:** The context is difficult conversations, with lessons from crisis counseling, not a controlled test of selling. Do not medicalize ordinary objections, diagnose a buyer, or mechanically repeat their last words as a persuasion trick.

### S4. Ethical obligations in marketing

American Marketing Association, *AMA Statement of Ethics*. Professional normative statement.

https://www.ama.org/ama-statement-of-ethics/

**Verified principle:** The statement emphasizes honesty, responsibility, equity, transparency, and citizenship. It rejects manipulation and coercion, calls for honoring commitments, and includes consideration for vulnerable audiences, privacy, material information, and conflicts of interest.

**Limits:** This is an aspirational professional code, not proof of business outcomes or a universal law. Agora can adopt stronger educational standards but should label them as its own design choices. “Legal” and “ethical” are different questions.

### S5. Claims require a basis before they are made

US Federal Trade Commission, *FTC Policy Statement Regarding Advertising Substantiation* (1984). Primary regulator policy.

https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/ftc-policy-statement-regarding-advertising-substantiation

**Verified principle:** Advertisers need a reasonable basis for objective express and implied claims before dissemination. What support is reasonable depends on the claim and context; saying a claim is proven by a particular kind of evidence commits the advertiser to that level of support.

**Limits:** This concerns US advertising policy. The course should teach evidential honesty, not promise jurisdiction-independent legal compliance. A hypothetical calculation, a limited pilot, and a guaranteed customer result are different kinds of claims.

### S6. Testimonials and disclosures cannot rescue every claim

US Federal Trade Commission, *Advertising FAQs: A Guide for Small Business*. Primary regulator guidance.

https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/advertising-faqs-guide-small-business

**Verified principle:** Testimonials ordinarily do not substantiate claims requiring objective evaluation; a money-back guarantee is not a substitute for evidence. Material qualifications should be understandable and noticeable. Fine print cannot simply contradict the main message or cure its misleading impression.

**Limits:** This broad guide includes specialized and evolving legal subjects. Use its stable truth-and-evidence principles; do not reproduce penalty figures or represent the course as legal advice. Teach what an ordinary reader is likely to understand, not only literal sentence truth.

### S7. Recognize manipulation at the decision point

US Federal Trade Commission, *FTC Report Shows Rise in Sophisticated Dark Patterns Designed to Trick and Trap Consumers* (2022). Agency summary of its staff report.

https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2022/09/ftc-report-shows-rise-sophisticated-dark-patterns-designed-trick-trap-consumers

**Verified principle:** Examples include false countdowns, hidden material terms or fees, disguised promotional content, difficult cancellation paths, and practices that manipulate privacy choices.

**Limits:** These are documented categories of potentially unlawful deceptive design, not a claim that all urgency or every awkward interface is illegal. An actual capacity constraint can be communicated truthfully; inventing one is different.

### S8. Questions for information, not merely rapport

Harvard Program on Negotiation, *Ask the Negotiation Coach: Questioning Negotiation Dialogue*. University expert guidance discussing research.

https://www.pon.harvard.edu/daily/negotiation-skills-daily/ask-the-negotiation-coach-nb/

**Verified principle:** Useful questioning includes relevant follow-ups and specific inquiries about material facts. The guidance warns about avoiding the question and using literally true statements to create misleading impressions.

**Limits:** Asking precisely does not guarantee an honest or complete reply. Appropriate specificity depends on the stage and relationship. Someone may legitimately decline to reveal confidential information; declining is not automatically evidence of wrongdoing.

### S9. Design agreements for changing conditions

Arvid Bell, Harvard Davis Center, *Make Your Agreements Crisis-Proof* (2020). University expert guidance.

https://daviscenter.fas.harvard.edu/insights/make-your-agreements-crisis-proof

**Verified principle:** Planning for changed circumstances, including contingent arrangements and joint problem solving, can make agreements more workable when conditions shift.

**Limits:** This is practical guidance, not an experimentally established guarantee. An if–then provision needs understandable triggers and consequences; vague contingencies can create new conflict. Some failures require ending an arrangement rather than preserving it at any cost.

## Four proposed units, three lessons each

The following is original curriculum design. Source IDs show the relevant foundation, not validation of the precise lesson. Each lesson can support a short concept card, two contrasting examples, several varied practice items, feedback, and a later transfer exercise. A three-to-five-minute target is a product choice to test with learners, not a scientifically established optimum.

## Scenario and exercise bank

Every “best” response below is best for the stated facts and learning objective. Some cases deliberately have multiple defensible choices. Avoid implying that one polite phrase guarantees agreement.

### 1.1 — “We need more placements”

**Situation:** Maya, a workforce-college director, says her team needs more graduate placements. You sell a fictional employer-outreach tool. You do not yet know whether the bottleneck is employer demand, student availability, or staff follow-up.

**Skill line:** Understand the problem before attaching your product to it.

**Strong response:** “Where are students getting stuck between finishing the program and starting work?” A defensible alternative is asking Maya to walk through one recent placement attempt.

**Tempting mistake:** Launching a feature tour because “placements” appears in the product description. The same word can hide different needs.

**Exercise:** Choose the next question, reveal Maya's answer, then select a different follow-up based on that answer. Do not reward a preselected script regardless of context.

**Ethics throughout:** Do not magnify fear about student outcomes to manufacture a need the product cannot address.

### 1.2 — “The evening schedule is the issue”

**Situation:** Luis is considering a training program. He says, “I want it, but evenings are hard because I collect my sister from work.” The recruiter replies, “So motivation is your obstacle.”

**Skill line:** Reflect the concern they expressed, and leave room to be corrected.

**Strong response:** “It sounds like the pickup time conflicts with class. Have I understood that?” Then ask about schedule options only if useful. Luis may choose not to explain further.

**Tempting mistake:** Calling him uncommitted or prying into family circumstances. Neither is required to establish whether the schedule works.

**Exercise:** Repair a mismatched paraphrase; distinguish acknowledgment (“That sounds difficult to arrange”) from agreement to an unavailable schedule change.

**Ethics throughout:** Relevant constraints are information, not vulnerabilities to exploit.

### 1.3 — “I like it, but I can't sign”

**Situation:** A college adviser likes your demonstration. Purchasing, accessibility, and student-services staff have requirements that have not been checked.

**Skill line:** A willing contact is not necessarily a complete decision process.

**Strong response:** “Who else needs to evaluate this, and what will they need to decide?” Offer an accessible summary the adviser can share. Another defensible option is pausing until a stated critical requirement is verified.

**Tempting mistake:** Asking the adviser to bypass purchasing or conceal the accessibility limitation until after signature.

**Exercise:** Sort questions into “needed for this decision,” “optional with a reason,” and “intrusive.” Budget range can be useful; demanding a secret maximum is not necessary.

**Ethics throughout:** Qualify the fit and process, not a person's worth or demographic desirability.

### 2.1 — “Automatic texts” is not yet value

**Situation:** Priya manages a small career-services team. She reports repeatedly missing students who cannot answer calls during shifts. Your tool can send scheduled messages, but you have not measured placement effects.

**Skill line:** Name the capability, connect it to the stated need, and preserve uncertainty.

**Strong response:** “Scheduled messages could give those students another way to respond. We could test response rates with a small group before expanding.” A manual alternative may be sufficient for a very small team.

**Tempting mistake:** “This guarantees more jobs for your students.” The capability does not establish the claimed outcome.

**Exercise:** Build a three-part explanation: observed problem → relevant capability → bounded expected benefit or test. Reject a link whose causal step is missing.

**Ethics throughout:** Do not borrow the emotional importance of jobs to inflate evidence.

### 2.2 — The five-person pilot

**Situation:** In an explicitly fictional pilot, four of five volunteers reported that a reminder was useful. There is no comparison group and no employment-outcome measurement. A draft headline says, “Proven to improve placement by 80%.”

**Skill line:** Keep the claim inside the evidence.

**Strong response:** Remove the placement claim. If relevant, state the limited pilot observation with its sample and what was measured. “We don't know yet” is an acceptable answer to a request for placement impact.

**Tempting mistake:** Keeping the headline and adding tiny “results vary” text. The central claim still exceeds the facts.

**Exercise:** Match four claims to their actual evidence; mark an illustrative estimate as an estimate; identify missing information before accepting a chart.

**Ethics throughout:** A favorable anecdote can describe one experience; it cannot silently become a typical or causal result.

### 2.3 — The competitor meets the requirement

**Situation:** Nia needs a tool compatible with her organization's required assistive technology. Yours has a known unresolved incompatibility. A competitor meets that requirement; you do not know all of its other capabilities.

**Skill line:** A fair comparison makes material tradeoffs visible.

**Strong response:** Disclose the incompatibility. Recommend evaluating the alternative against the remaining needs, or defer if Nia chooses to wait for a verified fix. Do not promise a release date the team has not committed to.

**Tempting mistake:** “Nobody else has complained,” which does not answer Nia's requirement.

**Exercise:** Choose between a narrower purchase, a delayed decision, and a referral under different stated constraints. More than one can be defensible when the facts permit it.

**Ethics throughout:** A graceful no-fit outcome is evidence of competent qualification.

### 3.1 — “It's expensive”

**Situation:** Devon says your proposed service is expensive. You do not know whether he lacks funds, doubts the benefit, is comparing different scopes, or simply wants to decline.

**Skill line:** Clarify before discounting or arguing.

**Strong response:** “Is the main concern the available budget, the value you expect, or something else?” If Devon says he has decided against it, accept the decision. A simple open “What concerns you most about the price?” is also defensible.

**Tempting mistake:** Immediately reducing price, or treating every objection as something to defeat.

**Exercise:** Respond to three different meanings behind the same initial sentence; identify when there is enough information to stop asking.

**Ethics throughout:** A buyer is not obliged to reveal finances or justify a no.

### 3.2 — A freelancer's sustainable offer

**Situation:** A fictional freelancer, Ren, quoted $1,200 for three deliverables. The client's stated limit is $800. Ren can sustainably offer two deliverables for that amount, or keep the original scope with a later payment schedule if appropriate for both parties.

**Skill line:** Change the exchange clearly, not only the number.

**Strong response:** Present feasible options with scope, dates, payment obligations, and consequences. Ren may also decline because neither option works.

**Tempting mistake:** Agreeing to the full workload while secretly intending to omit revisions, or shaming the client for having a budget.

**Exercise:** Assemble two complete offers from scope, timing, support, and price cards; flag an undisclosed tradeoff. Do not score a concession as automatically bad or good.

**Ethics throughout:** Fairness includes the seller's ability to deliver without self-exploitation.

### 3.3 — The Friday deadline

**Situation:** Your onboarding team has one genuine opening next week. It can hold that opening until Friday; future openings will remain available. A colleague suggests saying the entire offer disappears tonight.

**Skill line:** Explain the real constraint and its actual consequence.

**Strong response:** “We can hold next week's start until Friday. After that, we'd confirm a later available date.” If the timing does not suit the buyer, accept a later start.

**Tempting mistake:** Inventing a midnight deadline or implying the product will never be available again.

**Exercise:** Sort deadlines into verified constraint, unknown claim requiring a check, and fabricated pressure. Rewrite a misleading countdown message.

**Ethics throughout:** Urgency is information about a real choice, not permission to narrow it deceptively.

### 4.1 — A clear next step

**Situation:** Amara's workplace team agrees that a proposed pilot may help, but staff time, support, renewal, and who evaluates results are not yet clear.

**Skill line:** Ask for the smallest useful commitment with the relevant terms visible.

**Strong response:** Propose a defined pilot with owner, time commitment, price, success measures, review date, and what happens afterward. Invite correction before acceptance.

**Tempting mistake:** Calling it “free” while concealing mandatory setup fees or an automatic paid continuation.

**Exercise:** Find the missing commitment in a summary; choose a proportionate ask; distinguish a demo agreement from permission to bill.

**Ethics throughout:** A confident recommendation can coexist with a clear opportunity to decline.

### 4.2 — Follow up without pursuit

**Situation:** Sam says the team will review the proposal on Tuesday and asks for an email on Wednesday. A later message says, “We've decided not to proceed; please stop following up.”

**Skill line:** Follow the agreed purpose and respect withdrawal.

**Strong response:** Initially send the relevant Wednesday message. After withdrawal, acknowledge briefly and stop sales follow-up. Do not reinterpret silence or refusal as hidden permission.

**Tempting mistake:** A guilt-based “breakup email,” escalating to unrelated coworkers, or continuing a sequence because software scheduled it.

**Exercise:** Select follow-up content appropriate to an agreed decision date; repair an automated sequence after a stop request.

**Ethics throughout:** There is no evidence-based universal number of touches in this research. Teach relevance and permission, not a persistence quota.

### 4.3 — Repair the promise

**Situation:** You promised a data export in Friday's demonstration. On Monday, the team confirms it cannot be delivered on the agreed timeline. The customer has not discovered the error.

**Skill line:** Surface the error while the other person still has meaningful options.

**Strong response:** Correct the record promptly, explain the effect on the agreement, and offer feasible alternatives: verified workaround, revised scope or date, or an appropriate route to cancel or obtain a remedy under the agreement. Do not promise a remedy you cannot authorize.

**Tempting mistake:** Quietly changing the presentation, waiting for complaint, or making another unsupported promise to protect the first.

**Exercise:** Order the repair steps; draft a concise correction; identify which promised dates and owners require verification.

**Ethics throughout:** Delivery and repair are part of the exchange, not activities outside the seller's responsibility.

## Practical assessment design

1. **Separate factual from judgment questions.** “The pilot measured placement outcomes” can be unambiguously false. “Offer a narrower scope or decline” can have two defensible answers depending on capacity and goals. State those facts before grading.
2. **Explain the attractive mistake.** Feedback should identify the missing evidence, misunderstood concern, hidden condition, or pressure tactic. Avoid merely declaring a response “ethical” or “bad.”
3. **Ask for a second move.** After the learner picks a question, reveal an answer that makes them adapt. Reward updating, not memorizing a phrase.
4. **Practice repair.** Let learners correct a claim, acknowledge a misunderstood concern, or revise an agreement. A wrong first answer should create a learning opportunity rather than a moral label.
5. **Make no deal a valid outcome.** Some scenarios should be completed successfully by referring elsewhere, deferring, declining unsustainable work, or accepting a refusal.
6. **Transfer across roles.** Revisit the same principle as a founder, buyer, freelancer, candidate, and teammate. A candidate should never be taught that respectful negotiation requires revealing irrelevant personal information.
7. **Avoid pseudo-measurement.** A game may award practice points for noticing evidence and protecting choice. Do not present a “trust +10” or “chance of closing +30%” as a real prediction. If a fictional simulation uses such numbers, label its simplified model explicitly.
8. **Build an end-of-unit conversation.** Use several small decisions: listen, check a requirement, choose a supportable claim, make an offer, and respond to a no. Evaluate the reasoning visible in those decisions rather than rewarding the signature alone.

## Content-review checklist

- Is the answer warranted by the facts supplied, or does it assume hidden motives?
- Is the buyer's material information visible before the decision?
- Are numerical results explicitly fictional where invented, and supported where factual?
- Is an estimate distinguished from evidence and from a guarantee?
- Does the item permit refusal and legitimate confidentiality?
- Does the seller retain reasonable boundaries and a viable offer?
- Is a pressured, misleading, or invasive tactic ever rewarded as the sole winning path?
- Are multiple defensible responses acknowledged when appropriate?
- Does feedback explain a concrete consequence without inventing a guaranteed reaction?
- Are the sources described as research, guidance, or ethics codes accurately?

## Claims to exclude

Do not teach fixed talk/listen ratios, universal question counts, guaranteed closing scripts, unsupported percentages about objections, personality typing from a sentence, stereotypes about demographic bargaining styles, or claims that ethical conduct always maximizes profit. Do not equate empathy with agreement, confidence with proof, a testimonial with causal evidence, or a signed contract with a successful outcome.

## Post-review evidence correction

Independent review identified a published correction to S2 for confusing date offers given and received, affecting its results and conclusions. The original authors also published a reply. S2 must not be presented as settled corrective evidence against S1. The narrow Agora teaching remains: follow the answer, do not treat question counts as a sales formula, and distinguish listening from a scripted quota.
