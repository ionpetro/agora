# Agora: ethics and learning research

Research checked: 2 November 2026. This is a curriculum research brief, not legal advice or a claim that completing Agora establishes professional competence. All proposed characters, organizations, amounts, and outcomes below are fictional.

## Recommendation

Teach sales and negotiation as helping people make informed, workable agreements. Ethics belongs inside discovery, evidence, pricing, persuasion, bargaining, closing, and delivery. It should not appear only as a final lesson about being nice. A successful exercise can end in an agreement, a narrower pilot, more investigation, or an informed no.

Use four recurring questions: **Is it supported? Can they choose? Who bears the cost? Who will follow through?** These are Agora's proposed teaching prompts, not a validated ethical assessment. They deliberately leave room for competing interests and judgment. Knowing the right vocabulary does not establish that somebody acts ethically.

The four units below provide twelve lesson seeds that can be woven into a larger sales/negotiation path. They are not a recommendation to isolate ethics in a separate track.

## Source register and limits

The following sources were opened during research. Philosophical frameworks and institutional principles supply arguments and design criteria; experiments supply limited evidence about learning. Neither category proves that this course changes real selling behavior.

## Teachable distinctions

These are proposed instructional decisions informed by the sources above. They should be tested against learner explanations and reviewer disagreement.

| Topic | Decision to practise | Avoid teaching |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Truth and evidence | Match the strength of a claim to the available evidence. Specify sample, scope, uncertainty, and relevant exceptions when they change what the buyer would reasonably infer. Correct a misleading impression before relying on assent. | “A true sentence cannot mislead”; invented precision; anecdotes presented as typical results. |
| Omission and confidentiality | Separate information needed to understand the offer from private bargaining information. Disclose a known limitation that defeats the stated use. A seller need not automatically disclose their reservation price, and may truthfully decline to answer a confidential question. | “Ethics means reveal everything”; “anything unsaid is fair game”; claiming a confidential fact is false instead of declining to discuss it. |
| Autonomy and refusal | Make the proposed next step clear, bounded, and easy to decline. Distinguish an invitation to clarify an objection from continuing after an explicit refusal. | Treating every no as an obstacle to overcome, or treating one initial objection as a permanent ban on a respectful clarifying question. |
| Power and fairness | Notice who can wait, walk away, understand the terms, or absorb failure. Consider comparable criteria, accessible information, time, alternatives, and burdens. | Assuming voluntary assent settles fairness, every unequal deal is exploitative, or fairness always requires an equal split. |
| Emotion and urgency | Use accurate, relevant stories and explain genuine deadlines. Recognize guilt, fear, or dependency being exploited to bypass a considered choice. | “Emotion is manipulation”; “urgency is always fake”; deliberate shame dressed up as motivation. |
| Privacy and AI | Use only relevant, appropriately obtained information; avoid sensitive inferred vulnerabilities. Check AI-created claims against evidence and keep an accountable human owner. Explain consequential AI involvement when it matters to informed choice. | “Publicly visible means appropriate to exploit”; “the AI said it, so nobody is responsible”; an unsupported universal legal disclosure rule. |
| Accountability and repair | Make promises operational: who does what, by when, how success is checked, and how concerns are raised. When a material error appears, correct it and offer a workable remedy. | Promising that an apology restores trust, treating escalation as costless for a junior employee, or measuring success solely by signed contracts. |

### Where reasonable disagreement belongs

Reasonable people may disagree about the fairest price, how much risk each party should carry, which stakeholder deserves priority, when an emotional appeal crosses a line, and how much explanation a low-stakes decision needs. A firm boundary can be compatible with respect. A discount can be generous, unnecessary, or unfair to other buyers depending on the facts.

Agora should make the facts and assumed objective visible before asking for a “strongest next move.” Where two options meet the stated criteria, accept both with different tradeoff explanations or rewrite the question. Do not manufacture a single answer by making the ethical option longer, warmer, or more polite. Respectful language cannot repair a false claim.

## Four units × three lesson seeds

Each lesson should introduce one usable distinction, ask the learner to retrieve it, then change the surface story. A compact activity sequence is: ungraded prediction → one-breath explanation → two short decisions → a changed-context decision → reflection or retry. Session length is a product choice to test, not an established scientific optimum.

### Unit 1 — A yes worth having

1. **Say what the evidence says.** Goal: turn an attractive claim into a supported claim without making it useless. Exercise: match three claims to three evidence cards, then edit “Our training doubles everyone's salary” using a small, self-selected survey. Strong response explains what is known and uncertain. Transfer: a software uptime claim with a different measurement period.
2. **The missing line.** Goal: identify an omitted fact that changes fit or total commitment. Exercise: reveal a buyer's needs, then select which limitation must be brought forward. A reservation price is a deliberate distractor, so privacy is not equated with dishonesty. Transfer: subscription cancellation versus a product's missing accessibility feature.
3. **Let no mean no.** Goal: distinguish a question about fit from an explicit request to stop. Exercise: sort “Not this quarter,” “How would this help?”, and “Do not contact me again,” then choose an appropriate next step given the exact wording and prior contact. Transfer: a colleague declining an optional project.

**Unit review:** Can the learner support a claim, identify a decision-relevant limitation, and respond appropriately to refusal? Require one unfamiliar scenario for each. The reviewer must reject questions whose answer depends on assuming an unstated consent, deadline, or product capability.

### Unit 2 — Persuasion under pressure

1. **A story is not a statistic.** Goal: use an accurate example without presenting it as typical evidence. Exercise: choose a story-and-context pair; explain which inference the audience might draw. Transfer: a fundraiser using a vivid but atypical case.
2. **A real clock.** Goal: communicate a genuine constraint without inventing pressure. Exercise: compare a venue's fixed booking cutoff, a resetting sales timer, and a supplier deadline that can be extended at a cost. Transfer: a job offer with a real hiring schedule. Several actions may be defensible; fake urgency is not needed to create a challenge.
3. **Who can walk away?** Goal: identify power asymmetry and one concrete improvement to the process. Exercise: place time, alternatives, information, and downside-risk cards beside two fictional parties. Choose a safeguard compatible with a firm business limit. Transfer: a novice freelancer negotiating with an experienced buyer.

**Unit review:** Can the learner distinguish emotion from evidential support, genuine time pressure from fabrication, and unequal power from a complete moral verdict? Reviewers must see at least one ethical firm boundary and one superficially friendly but manipulative option.

### Unit 3 — Fair dealing

1. **Fair does not always mean half.** Goal: compare defensible fairness criteria. Exercise: allocate a limited training budget using equal access, need, or expected benefit, then name who benefits and who loses under the chosen criterion. This is a reasoned dilemma, not a single-answer personality test. Transfer: a supplier discount based on volume and service costs.
2. **Private is not false.** Goal: protect confidential information while avoiding fabricated leverage. Exercise: select a truthful response when asked about a walk-away price or competing offer. Transfer: a buyer asking for another customer's confidential terms. Feedback should make room for declining to answer and proposing public benchmarks.
3. **Trade, do not corner.** Goal: explore differences in priorities while protecting informed agreement. Exercise: match requests to feasible packages of price, timing, scope, and support; reject options that hide costs or promise unauthorized work. Transfer: an employee negotiating schedule flexibility. “No feasible agreement” must sometimes be a correct diagnosis.

**Unit review:** Can the learner state a fairness criterion, separate confidentiality from deception, and check package feasibility for both parties? Reviewers must not equate accepting the middle price with sound reasoning or imply that mutual gains always exist.

### Unit 4 — Trust after the handshake

1. **The AI wrote it. You own the claim.** Goal: review an AI-generated outreach message for unsupported claims and intrusive personalization. Exercise: select evidence to verify each claim and remove an inferred sensitive trait. Transfer: an AI-written proposal with an invented customer endorsement. Any data shown is synthetic.
2. **A promise with a name and a date.** Goal: make an agreement executable. Exercise: assemble scope, owner, deadline, success check, and escalation route; identify which promise exceeds authority. Transfer: a buyer's internal approval dependency.
3. **Repair before defending.** Goal: respond to an error without concealment or empty reassurance. Exercise: order acknowledgment, accurate correction, affected-party notification, feasible options, and follow-up in a case where the timing is explicit. Transfer: a service failure after an initially honest sale. Where sequence depends on safety or urgency, state those facts.

**Unit review:** Can the learner verify an AI claim, respect a data boundary, assign ownership, and offer proportionate repair? Reviewers must include a realistic organizational constraint and must not reward shifting blame to software or making an impossible compensation promise.

## Grading ethical reasoning without grading the person

Use three different assessment modes; make the mode visible in authoring data and feedback.

| Mode | Appropriate use | Feedback and reward |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fact or defined distinction | Given facts establish a claim's evidence, an explicit stop request, a stated cost, or the definition taught. | Correct/incorrect is appropriate. Explain the decisive fact and requeue missed retrieval. |
| Conditional best action | The prompt gives an objective, known facts, authority, and constraints. One action better meets those criteria, or several are explicitly accepted. | “Stronger under these facts because…” Name the tradeoff and a fact that would change the answer. Do not say “You are ethical/unethical.” |
| Open dilemma | Legitimate values conflict and available information does not determine one winner. | Credit participation and reasoning steps: identify a stakeholder, cite a relevant fact, explain a tradeoff, ask for missing information, or revise. Do not treat the author's preferred allocation as moral truth. |

For a choice-based engine that cannot interpret open reasoning, use **choose a position → select a supporting reason → reveal a counterconsideration**. Grade an explicitly false supporting claim if needed, but do not secretly mark the position itself wrong. If the engine supports only one right answer, keep unresolved dilemmas as ungraded reflections.

Practice XP should reflect participation, retrieval, review, and completion. Optional skill markers can record demonstrated actions such as “checked a claim” or “identified an affected party.” Do not combine them into an ethics percentage, virtue rank, trustworthiness prediction, or employee-selection score. A deterministic “trust meter” can teach false certainty; show a particular character's stated reaction and alternative plausible reactions instead.

### Content review rubric — apply to every unit

Mark each criterion **pass**, **revise**, or **not applicable with reason**. These are publishing gates for the content, not judgments of the learner.

1. **Supported facts:** factual explanation has provenance; the source supports the exact claim and is not stretched into law, causality, or a universal rule.
2. **Answer type:** the author identifies fact, conditional action, or open dilemma. Conditional questions state the objective and enough constraints to discriminate options.
3. **Honest alternatives:** distractors are plausible; at least one is tempting for a real commercial reason. A second defensible answer is accepted or the scenario is repaired.
4. **Truth and omissions:** claims match evidence; important caveats are specific; confidentiality is not automatically condemned.
5. **Agency and power:** learners can identify a meaningful choice, explicit refusal, and relevant dependency without assuming every unequal relationship invalidates agreement.
6. **Consequences and fairness:** more than the seller's revenue matters. Outcomes are plausible and acknowledge uncertainty; ethical conduct is not guaranteed to win business.
7. **Data and AI:** data is synthetic, permission and purpose are specified where relevant, and a human remains responsible for verification and follow-through.
8. **Executable response:** the preferred move can actually be performed within the character's role, authority, time, and resources. Repair and escalation have concrete owners.
9. **Learning quality:** at least one question requires the concept in a new context; surface wording does not reveal the answer; wrong-answer feedback explains rather than scolds.
10. **Dignity and inclusion:** characters are not moral stereotypes; culture is not inferred from a name; assertiveness, accessibility needs, and imperfect English are not proxies for competence or ethics.

Suggested review process: a subject reviewer checks factual and conceptual accuracy; an adversarial learner attempts to defend every rejected answer; a final editor resolves disagreements and checks that branches preserve choice and consequence. Keep a short change log of resolved ambiguity.

## Four branching negotiation roleplays

Each roleplay needs at least one returnable branch, a changed fact, and a debrief tied to a specific decision. Learners should sometimes switch roles. Reactions below are authored possibilities, not empirical predictions of how people must behave.

### 1. The pilot with the impressive headline

**Role:** seller of a fictional career-training pilot. **Counterpart:** a small employer with a limited training budget. **Known facts:** a survey of 12 volunteers found 8 reporting improved interview confidence; there is no placement study. The buyer needs evidence of job placement. The pilot can measure interview performance but cannot guarantee employment.

- **Opening choice:** claim “Most graduates get hired,” state the actual survey finding and its limits, or pause to investigate. The unsupported claim can create initial interest, but an evidence request exposes the mismatch. Investigation delays the discussion without necessarily losing it.
- **Buyer asks for a guarantee:** offer an unauthorized hiring guarantee, clarify the desired outcome and propose measurable pilot goals, or explain that the requested result cannot be promised. The latter two can both be defensible; clarify what each sacrifices.
- **Changed fact:** onboarding requires more manager time than the buyer has. Reveal the requirement and negotiate smaller scope, conceal it until signing, or stop the sale. The concealed route leads to a delivery problem; learners can still correct the omission and reopen consent.
- **Endings:** transparent, feasible pilot; buyer pauses for evidence; honest no-fit; or a strained agreement requiring repair. No ranking by revenue alone.

**Debrief:** Which claim exceeded the evidence? What information affected fit? What would need to be true to support a stronger future claim? **Role switch:** the buyer identifies the minimum evidence needed, without demanding impossible certainty.

### 2. The offer expires Friday

**Role:** candidate negotiating a job offer. **Counterpart:** a recruiter with a fixed approved salary range and a genuine Friday staffing decision. **Known facts:** the candidate has no competing offer; flexibility matters more than a small salary increase. The recruiter can request an exception but cannot promise one.

- **Opening choice:** invent a competing offer, ask about the range and priorities, or state a reasoned salary request using relevant experience. Both truthful routes can advance the negotiation.
- **Deadline branch:** accept immediately, request a specific extension with a reason, or decline if the uncertainty is unacceptable. An extension may be refused for a real constraint; refusal is not proof of malicious pressure.
- **Changed fact:** salary is fixed, but a later start date and one remote day are negotiable. Explore a package, demand disclosure of confidential coworkers' salaries, or decide the role still does not meet the candidate's needs.
- **Endings:** a workable package, a respectful no-deal, or time to consider at the cost of losing this opening. The invented-offer route includes a chance to correct the false statement; do not require miraculous forgiveness.

**Debrief:** What information was private? Which claim would have been false? Who had more ability to wait? **Role switch:** the recruiter communicates the real constraint without inventing consequences or belittling hesitation.

### 3. The follow-up that went too far

**Role:** sales adviser offering a fictional professional workshop. **Counterpart:** someone who requested details and has since said, “I cannot decide this week; please email me next Monday.” **Known facts:** two seats remain, but another cohort opens next month. No permission exists to share their contact details with a partner.

- **Opening choice:** respect the requested timing, send a guilt-based message about commitment, or contact the person's employer. The last two may get attention while undermining the stated boundary; attention is not treated as success.
- **Monday branch:** explain the two current seats and next cohort, imply that this is their final chance ever, or ask whether they still want to discuss fit. Both transparent routes remain available.
- **Changed fact:** the counterpart volunteers a transport barrier and asks about remote participation. Offer accurate alternatives or ask permission before making a specific introduction; do not infer income, diagnosis, or willingness to share information.
- **Refusal branch:** if they say “Please stop contacting me,” stop outreach. If they choose a later cohort, record the agreed contact preference rather than enrolling them automatically.

**Endings:** enrollment that fits, permission for one defined follow-up, referral with consent, or a respected no. **Debrief:** What did the person authorize, for what purpose, and when? **Role switch:** the learner practices setting a clear boundary without having to disclose personal reasons.

### 4. The AI promise nobody can deliver

**Role:** agency account manager. **Counterpart:** a buyer about to sign a proposal. **Known facts:** an AI-assisted draft promises a two-week integration; the delivery team says four weeks is the earliest credible date. A manager wants this quarter's sale. The account manager can adjust scope, but needs approval for a refund or price concession.

- **Discovery branch:** quietly change the internal plan, correct the buyer's expectation before signature, or confirm feasibility with the delivery owner first. Verification is useful only if the buyer is not allowed to rely on the known bad promise in the meantime.
- **Negotiation branch:** offer a feasible two-week partial milestone plus four-week completion, keep the unsupported promise, or pause the purchase. Ask what deadline actually matters before prescribing the package.
- **Changed fact:** the buyer's launch truly requires the full integration in two weeks. Acknowledge the mismatch, explore an alternative supplier or scope if appropriate, or let the buyer decline. Do not frame nonpurchase as irrational.
- **After-sale variant:** the error is discovered after signature. Correct the record, identify affected work, escalate to an authorized owner, and offer concrete options the agency can honor. Avoid claiming that “the AI made the mistake” ends responsibility.

**Endings:** revised agreement, cancelled negotiation, or agreed repair with an owner and check-in. **Debrief:** Who verified the promise, who could authorize changes, and which organizational incentive contributed? **Role switch:** the buyer chooses among imperfect remedies; they need not forgive to complete the exercise.

## Learning and transfer plan

Agora should borrow the existing short-session, retrieval, spaced-review, mistake-retry, and varied-activity workflow while testing its assumptions. Start with a manageable example, then change industry, role, stakes, or evidence. Repeating the exact wording primarily tests recognition. A learner who memorizes “ask an open question” still needs to decide when another question would disrespect an explicit refusal.

Use one delayed unfamiliar scenario per unit and a later mixed review. Preserve the family's existing spaced schedule as a starting product convention; these sources do not establish that its exact intervals are optimal. Optional speed rounds should test clear factual distinctions, not rush sensitive dilemmas or reward snap moral judgments.

Evaluate learning separately from engagement:

- **Immediate:** can learners identify the decisive fact and explain why a tempting option fails?
- **Delayed:** can they apply the principle to an unseen situation without the original cue wording?
- **Transfer:** can they revise an initial answer after relevant new information and distinguish their preferred outcome from what the other person may choose?
- **Product:** completion, voluntary return, and perceived usefulness describe engagement; none alone proves learning or ethical behavior.

For an early pilot, collect a small set of anonymized explanations and have two reviewers independently check observable reasoning steps: supported facts, affected parties, tradeoffs, missing information, and revision. Report disagreement and refine ambiguous questions. Do not claim that a pre/post improvement demonstrates causality without a suitable comparison, or that simulated choices establish workplace conduct. Keep realistic practice valuable even when those larger claims remain unproven.
