# Agora: negotiation research

Research checked 2026-11-02. This file supports content design; it is not a promise of negotiation outcomes. The source ledger distinguishes research abstracts, university teaching, and expert advice. All scenarios and numbers below are newly constructed, hypothetical teaching examples, not Job Machine results or market benchmarks.

## Evidence and source ledger

All nine linked pages were opened and their relevant text inspected. No commercial case text or exercise has been copied.

1. **N1 — Preparation as seven interacting elements.** [MIT Sloan, Seven keys to effective negotiation](https://mitsloan.mit.edu/ideas-made-to-matter/seven-keys-to-effective-negotiation), February 17, 2016. University report of Jared Curhan's teaching: consider stakeholders/interests, alternatives, options, legitimacy, commitments, relationships, and communication. These are preparation considerations, not a mandatory sequence. The framework supports asking who else is affected and which criteria could justify terms. This is a teaching framework, not a causal evaluation of every element.

2. **N2 — BATNA and ZOPA.** [Harvard Business School Online, Understanding ZOPA: The Zone of Possible Agreement](https://online.hbs.edu/blog/post/understanding-zopa), Marcela Merino, September 14, 2017; updated February 18, 2020. BATNA is the action taken if negotiation fails. Agreement is possible where acceptable terms overlap; under fixed terms, incompatible limits mean no ZOPA. Counterpart limits usually remain uncertain, and the zone may change as priorities change. This university explainer supports the concepts, not a guarantee that preparation produces agreement or that acceptance proves fairness.

3. **N3 — First offers and anchoring.** [Harvard Program on Negotiation, When to Make the First Offer in Negotiations](https://www.pon.harvard.edu/daily/dealmaking-daily/resolving-the-first-offer-dilemma-in-business-negotiations/), September 28, 2026. First numbers can influence subsequent judgments. The page distinguishes an alternative, a target, and a reservation point and discusses information as a condition for making an opening offer. It summarizes negotiation research rather than reporting a new study. Do not turn its advice into “always go first,” “never go first,” or a guarantee that an extreme offer succeeds. Its decision table is guidance, not a validated decision algorithm.

4. **N4 — Cooperation, competition, and power.** [Stanford Report, How to be a better negotiator](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2024/10/lets-make-deal-negotiation-tips-experts), October 8, 2024. Interviews with Stanford faculty describe negotiation as interdependent decision making containing cooperative and competitive elements. Priorities can differ, making trades possible. Alternatives, expertise, relationships, and formal authority can affect power. This is expert synthesis, not evidence that every bargain can become a win-win. The course should distinguish expanding possible value from deciding how to divide it.

5. **N5 — Multiple equivalent simultaneous offers (MESOs).** [Columbia Business School, Putting more on the table: How making multiple offers can increase the final value of the deal](https://business.columbia.edu/faculty/research/putting-more-table-how-making-multiple-offers-can-increase-final-value-deal), V.H. Medvec and Adam Galinsky, *Negotiation* 4, 2005, pp. 3–5. The accessible university research entry contains an abstract, not the complete article. It describes possible economic and relational benefits of offering multiple packages. MESOs are equivalent in the proposer's valuation; they need not be equivalent to the recipient. Do not infer a universal effect size or optimal number of packages from this abstract.

6. **N6 — MESOs do not establish fairness.** [Harvard Program on Negotiation, The Negotiator's Dilemma: How MESOs Help You Create and Claim Value](https://www.pon.harvard.edu/daily/negotiation-skills-daily/managing-the-negotiators-dilemma-nb/), July 7, 2026. This guide summarizes Leonardelli and colleagues' negotiation simulations. Offerers did better with multiple equivalent packages; recipients perceived more cooperation, yet received slightly less than recipients of a single first offer. That asymmetry matters: apparent flexibility is not proof of a fair deal. This evidence supports teaching recipients to compare the offered menu with their own alternatives and criteria. Results from simulations do not establish what will happen in every real negotiation.

7. **N7 — Difficult conversations.** [Harvard Program on Negotiation, Telling the Third Story](https://www.pon.harvard.edu/daily/conflict-resolution/telling-the-third-story/), August 4, 2026, drawing on Douglas Stone, Bruce Patton, and Sheila Heen. A useful opening describes a disagreement in a way both sides can recognize, then invites their accounts. This is a practical conversational approach, not an experiment demonstrating guaranteed de-escalation. Agora should not imply that a neutral-sounding sentence establishes the truth, makes responsibility equal, or replaces appropriate responses to coercion or misconduct.

8. **N8 — Contingencies and their limits.** [Harvard Program on Negotiation, Set Your Contingent Contract Up for Success](https://www.pon.harvard.edu/daily/dealmaking-daily/set-your-contingent-contract-up-for-success/), October 26, 2026. Future-dependent terms can accommodate different forecasts, but vague measures, excessive optimism, and poorly distributed risk can undermine them. Terms need clear triggers, monitoring, and an implementation owner. This is expert guidance using examples, not proof that every disagreement calls for a bet. Declining a contingency does not prove dishonesty: risk tolerance, cash constraints, or uncontrollable outcomes may explain refusal. Agora uses its own scenarios rather than the article's news example.

9. **N9 — Listening and workable commitments.** [Harvard Medical School Ombuds, Seven Elements of Effective Negotiations](https://hms.harvard.edu/sites/default/files/assets/Sites/Ombuds/files/HMS.HSDM_.HSPH%20Ombuds%20Office.Seven%20Elements%20of%20Effective%20Negotiations.pdf), Jerome Slavik, December 2008, adapted from *Getting to Yes*. The three-page practitioner handout recommends paraphrasing, clarifying interests, considering the costs and risks of alternatives, and planning implementation. It evaluates outcomes partly by feasibility and fairness. Source selection is critical: its suggestion to worsen another party's alternative is deliberately **not** adopted. Agora teaches strengthening one's own options and fair process, not sabotaging another person's ability to refuse.

## Precise distinctions for writers

| Concept | Meaning in Agora | Common confusion to prevent |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Position / interest | A requested term / the concern or purpose behind it. | An inferred motive is a hypothesis until checked. |
| BATNA | Best feasible course of action available without this agreement. | It is not an opening demand or a minimum price. A hoped-for offer is not a secured alternative. |
| Reservation point | Boundary at which the complete deal is as attractive as the alternative, given the person's constraints and judgments. | It is not automatically last salary, production cost, or target. Non-price terms matter. |
| Target | Outcome one aims for. | It can be more ambitious than the minimum; neither is a claim about market truth. |
| ZOPA | Set of agreements acceptable to all relevant negotiating parties. | An overlap is possible, not guaranteed, fair, or necessarily known. A price interval assumes other terms are fixed. |
| Distributive / integrative | Dividing existing value / searching for options or trades that improve what is available. | Negotiations can involve both. Joint gains still leave a distribution question. |
| Objective criterion | A relevant external standard that parties can examine and discuss. | A cherry-picked comparison or historical practice does not become fair merely by being measurable. |
| MESOs | Several packages offered together, equally attractive to the proposer under stated preferences. | “Three prices” is not automatically a MESO; a decoy is not a genuine choice. |
| Contingent term | A defined action or payment conditional on a future event. | It does not remove uncertainty, make every risk acceptable, or guarantee enforceability. |

Definitions synthesize N1–N6 and N8–N9. The qualifications concerning fairness and honest choice are Agora's normative design commitments, not claims that ethical behavior always pays financially.

## Proposed course arc: four units, three lessons each

These are design suggestions, not findings tested in the cited studies. Each lesson should move from a small recognition exercise to a choice in context, then a brief explanation and a transfer prompt. Ethical constraints belong inside the decision, not in a separate optional module.

### Unit 1 — Before you bargain

1. **What are we really solving?** Learn positions versus interests (N1, N9). A college asks for a cheaper program, but the stated concern is uncertain participation. Choose a question about enrollment and funding constraints before offering a discount. Distinguish a confirmed interest from a plausible guess. Wrong choices should include premature problem-solving as well as aggressive selling. Ethical thread: learners and delivery staff are stakeholders even if absent from the call.
2. **Your plan B is a plan.** Distinguish BATNA, target, and reservation point (N2, N3). Sort “accept a confirmed role,” “ask for $86,000,” and “do not accept below my evaluated threshold” into the right categories. A better outside option can support a boundary; inventing one cannot. Transfer: write one action that would make a real alternative more feasible.
3. **Is there room for agreement?** Use Case A below to calculate an overlap, then remove knowledge of the employer's limit. The correct next move changes from arithmetic to information gathering. Include a no-overlap problem where ending talks or changing scope is reasonable. Ethical thread: there is no requirement to rescue every deal by pushing the less powerful party below a workable limit.

### Unit 2 — Build a better offer

4. **More than one number.** Recognize distributive and integrative features (N4). In Case B, compare identical-price bargaining with scope, timing, and revision options. Ask which priority difference could create a trade before assuming a fixed pie. Ethical thread: extra work must have an owner who can actually consent and deliver.
5. **An anchor needs a reason.** Evaluate evidence for an opening proposal (N3). With thin information, select an exploratory question; with comparable scope and reliable benchmarks, formulate a supported offer. On receiving an extreme opening, pause and return to criteria and one's own evaluation. Ethical thread: do not invent competing bids, false market data, fake deadlines, or a fabricated “lowest possible price.”
6. **Offer a real choice.** Construct the two packages in Case C using an explicit cost model (N5), then inspect a menu from the buyer's side (N6). Ask whether any offer meets the buyer's constraints, not merely which looks best. Ethical thread: alternatives are all genuine, exclusions are visible, and recipients may counteroffer or reject the menu.

### Unit 3 — Use power responsibly

7. **Firm without a bluff.** Distinguish an honest boundary, a threat, a preference, and a misleading claim. Practise “I can discuss scope, but I cannot offer this package at that price” when it is true. Privacy about one's minimum is compatible with honesty; pretending a negotiable preference is an immutable fact is different. Ethical thread: improving alternatives and obtaining support are legitimate; engineered dependency and retaliation are not course success conditions. N4 informs power; these boundaries are course commitments.
8. **The conversation under the argument.** For Case D, separate observation, interpretation, and impact, then ask a focused question (N7, N9). Do not treat an unsupported guess about intent as a fact. Offer a pause or support route when participation is unsafe. Ethical thread: acknowledging someone's experience does not require endorsing every account or erasing documented responsibility.
9. **Who gets a voice?** A college partnership fits both signatories' budgets but burdens students and advisers. Identify missing stakeholders, decision authority, realistic review time, and a relevant standard (N1). No claim that “both signed” proves a fair process. Ethical thread: assess effects on people without bargaining power; challenge a biased precedent rather than laundering it as an objective benchmark.

### Unit 4 — Make the agreement work

10. **Agree about uncertainty.** Identify the trigger, measurement, and response in Case E (N8). Compare a clear staged pilot with “pay us if it works.” Recognize when the outcome is too hard to measure or largely outside one party's control. Ethical thread: confidence is not unlimited risk capacity; refusing a bet is not proof of lying.
11. **A yes that can be delivered.** Repair an incomplete agreement: who does what, by when, under which acceptance criteria, with what approval and change process (N9). Distinguish exploratory interest from authorized commitment. Ethical thread: disclose known dependencies and limitations before commitment. A written summary supports understanding; the lesson must not claim that every summary is a legally sufficient contract.
12. **A good no, a better next time.** Mixed capstone with an acceptable agreement, a weak alternative, and an unresolved stakeholder harm. Sometimes the best supported choice is a pause, changed scope, or respectful exit. Review preparation, listening, accuracy, and follow-through separately from whether a deal happened. The transfer task is a real preparation note or roleplay debrief, not a demand to secure a concession.

## Original numeric and dialogue cases

All figures, costs, preferences, organizations, and events in this section are hypothetical. They are exercise inputs, not advice about appropriate salaries or prices.

**Case A — Salary under fixed terms.** Rowan has evaluated benefits, commute, risk, and the alternative job, and decides the minimum acceptable annual salary for the new role is $80,000. Rowan's target is $86,000. In an omniscient exercise, the employer's maximum is $88,000, with every other term fixed. ZOPA: $80,000–$88,000, including the endpoints if both parties accept at indifference. BATNA: take the other confirmed job; not “$80,000.” A second version hides the employer's maximum: the learner must label the ZOPA **unknown**, not infer it from the first offer. A third version gives the employer a genuine $78,000 maximum: no overlap under these terms. Another term may change the comparison, but it must be evaluated rather than assumed to solve it.

**Case B — A service contract with a genuine limit.** A freelancer's minimum for the specified full scope is $6,000; the client's maximum is $5,000. Splitting the gap at $5,500 violates both limits. A reduced scope has a freelancer minimum of $4,500 and still satisfies the client's core need: its possible price range is $4,500–$5,000. The learner must confirm that reduced scope works for the client. Do not label the lower price “the same deal”; the deliverables changed. Any work removed should appear explicitly in the summary.

**Case C — Two packages, equal proposer value.** A studio evaluates only stated delivery costs and revenue in this simplified exercise; it has equal capacity for both schedules. Package A: $6,000, delivery in four weeks, two revision rounds, estimated cost $4,000. Package B: $6,600, delivery in three weeks, two revision rounds, estimated cost $4,600. Both produce $2,000 estimated contribution. Thus they are equivalent **under this studio's stated model**, not universally equivalent. The buyer prefers a faster launch but must compare whether one week is worth $600 to them. Neither package includes unlimited changes. Prompt: “Which part fits best, and what would you change?” Buyer-side transfer: a menu can still omit a better alternative; ask about it.

**Case D — A missed internal deadline.** Two of three agreed handoffs arrived a day late; the launch lead has had to rebook review time. Tempting response: “You do not care about our work.” Better supported opening: “Our last two handoffs arrived a day after the dates on the plan, and I had to move review time. I want to understand what changed and agree on dates we can both use.” Ask whether requirements or priorities shifted. If the facts are disputed, examine the shared record. Do not promise that this sentence makes the colleague cooperative. If a manager imposed incompatible deadlines, renegotiating priorities may be more appropriate than blaming the contributor.

**Case E — A college pilot with staged capacity.** A provider and college are unsure whether 20 or 50 people will opt into a four-week workshop. A hypothetical proposal reserves capacity for 20 at $2,000; if at least 35 people register by a stated date, both sides can approve a second session for $1,000 more, with an agreed maximum of 50 participants. What is missing? Define registration, consent, who verifies the count, when approval is due, and what happens to participants beyond capacity. Do not let a provider's payment depend on pressuring students to enroll. Another valid choice is a smaller pilot plus review, if the parties cannot responsibly commit to expansion. No claimed employment or learning outcome is part of this example.

## Content review checks

- A correct choice must follow from facts present in the prompt. When multiple reasonable goals conflict, ask for the most useful next step or explain tradeoffs; do not pretend one script is universally optimal.
- If a limit is “hard,” say who has authority and what establishes it. If it is estimated, say so. Update when new facts appear.
- Never reward deception because the fictional counterpart fell for it. Counterpart compliance does not establish informed consent.
- Do not equate courage with accepting risk someone cannot afford. Do not shame a learner for having a weak BATNA, needing a job, choosing support, or ending a conversation.
- Anchor exercises need comparable quantities and stated uncertainty. A large number alone is not evidence of value or unfairness.
- Assess listening by whether a response accurately checks meaning, not by whether it contains magic words such as “empathy” or “win-win.”
- Treat confidentiality and honesty separately. A learner can decline to reveal a reservation point without inventing facts.
- Avoid cultural or demographic scripts. Ask about the particular person's preferences, communication access, constraints, and decision process.
- Do not score virtue, trustworthiness, or future sales from one click. XP can reflect practising the skill; narrative consequences should be possibilities rather than guaranteed predictions.
- The capstone should include a justified no-deal outcome, a repaired mistake, and a later implementation check. Agreement alone is too narrow a success measure.

## Suggested assessment and transfer

Suggested progression: identify a concept → distinguish two close cases → choose a question → compare packages → explain a boundary → repair a commitment. Reuse concepts in different domains, not only different names and numbers. In a delayed exercise, hide the label “BATNA” and ask what the person would actually do without this agreement. Include a brief optional roleplay with observable criteria: states uncertainty honestly, checks an interest, proposes a feasible option, respects a refusal, and summarizes responsibilities. These are design recommendations; the cited material does not validate Agora's learning outcomes.
